Four first steps

Choose one manageable action in each area. You can learn at your own pace.

1. Read two newsletters

Subscribe to TLDR InfoSec for short daily headlines and SANS NewsBites for security news and practitioner commentary. Both are already in our Feeds section. Read a few stories a week and look up unfamiliar terms.

2. Learn by doing

Use Cisco Skills for All for introductory networking and cybersecurity courses. Then try a beginner lesson in the free PortSwigger Web Security Academy; it provides intentionally vulnerable web labs.

3. Meet your community

Find an event through ISC2 Philadelphia, ISSA Delaware Valley, or OWASP Philadelphia. Introduce yourself as a student and ask how people got started. Check whether each event welcomes students and whether there is a fee.

4. Try a CTF

A capture-the-flag (CTF) is a collection of puzzles where you find and submit a proof called a flag. Start with free, self-paced challenges at CyLab Security Academy (formerly picoCTF's practice platform). Use CTFtime to find upcoming events; read their descriptions, rules, dates, and eligibility to choose a beginner-friendly one.

Your first month

A suggested path, not a deadline. An hour or two each week is enough to start.

Week 1: Get oriented

Subscribe to TLDR InfoSec and SANS NewsBites, then start Cisco's free Introduction to Cybersecurity. For example, read one headline and write down what happened, who was affected, and one term to look up.

Week 2: Build foundations

Continue the Cisco course and try Ubuntu's beginner command-line tutorial on a Linux system or lab you own. For example, use pwd to show your location and ls to list files, then explain DNS in your own words.

Week 3: Practice

Try an apprentice lesson in PortSwigger's server-side learning path, or choose an introductory challenge at CyLab Security Academy. For example, use a hint, then write down the puzzle's goal, what you tried, and one thing you learned. Do not share private flags or others' answers.

Week 4: Connect & explore

Find a meeting in the Philadelphia groups directory or ask about a campus club. Use CyberSeek to explore a role such as security analyst, and ask your campus career center about an IT or security internship. For example, note two skills from a real posting and choose one to practice next.

What should I learn first?

You do not need to master everything before trying a lab. Build these skills gradually alongside your classes.

Networks & the web

Start Cisco's free Networking Basics course to learn about IP addresses and DNS. For example, sketch how your browser uses DNS to find a website and connects to its server.

Systems & identity

Try the beginner command-line tutorial in a system or lab you own, and read CISA's guide to multifactor authentication. For example, identify where to enable MFA on your own school or email account.

Risk & communication

Explore the NIST Cybersecurity Framework to see how organizations organize risk management. For example, explain how a lost laptop could expose student records and suggest encryption as one protection.

Scripts & curiosity

Explore introductory Python courses on Cisco Skills for All; you can begin cyber before knowing how to code. For example, write a short script that prints a reminder to update your software.

Turn learning into experience

Ask your campus career center and professors about student clubs, internships, IT help desk, and research opportunities. Keep a short portfolio of permitted lab write-ups and class projects; never publish credentials or sensitive data.

Certifications can be useful later, but do not assume you need to buy one now. Check employer requirements, student discounts, exam costs, and renewal rules before deciding.

Find your people

Ask a classmate to join you at a campus cyber or IT club, a local chapter meeting, or a CTF. Not near Philadelphia? Search for an OWASP chapter or cybersecurity group near your school.

Practice safely

Only test systems you own or have explicit permission to assess. Stay inside the rules and scope of each lab, CTF, or program; a public website is not automatically a practice target. Do not upload passwords, school or work data, or sensitive information to public tools.
